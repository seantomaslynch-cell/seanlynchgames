Hardwood GM player development guide: mentors and focus

Hardwood GM is a free, text-based basketball general manager sim. Every summer your players change. Some grow, some hold and some fade. This guide explains what drives that change, how minutes and mentors help young players, what summer focus does, why potential moves, and how decline and retirement work.

How a summer works

  • Development happens once a year, at the rollover. Each player has a peak age, set when he is created. Most peak ages fall between 24 and 32. Below that age he is in the growth phase. From his peak age through two years after it he is in the peak phase. After that he is in the decline phase.
  • Before his peak, the game closes the gap between his overall and his potential. The gap is spread over the years he has left. Growth front loads, so a 20 year old with a big gap gains faster than a 25 year old with the same gap.
  • After his peak there is a short plateau. Heavy legs shorten it. A player who has played a lot of career minutes hits decline sooner. The plateau starts at about two years and shrinks with mileage. After it ends, the yearly drop starts small and grows. Age 32 is a small step down. Age 36 is a cliff.
  • Every change has some luck in it. The game adds a random swing around the expected number, a little wider for young players. So two prospects with the same profile can end up in different places.
  • The change in overall is spread across the twelve ratings. Your own players show an outlook line on the player card, such as still improving, at his best, or past it.

What grows and what fades

  • Skills grow faster than bodies. When a young player improves, the points land most often in feel for the game, three point shooting, midrange, passing and handling. Athleticism is mostly given and grows the least.
  • Decline works the other way around. Athleticism goes first, then finishing and stamina. Feel for the game is the most durable rating, followed by passing, three point shooting, midrange and handling. This is why a 34 year old with a jumper and a high feel for the game can still help, and a 34 year old athlete often cannot.
  • Which ratings move also depends on position. A point's chance of landing on a rating rises with how much his position uses that rating.

Minutes are the currency

  • For a young player, the expected gain is scaled by an opportunity factor: 0.4 plus 0.8 times his minutes share. Minutes share is his minutes per game divided by 36, kept between 0.1 and 1. If he has no stats it defaults to 0.3. A prospect who never plays gets the low end.
  • A season in your G League affiliate counts too. If his affiliate minutes share is higher than his NBA share, the game uses the higher one. Sending a prospect down to play big minutes can beat leaving him on your bench.
  • His growth is also scaled by his own development speed, a hidden number that differs from player to player.
  • Traits add or subtract. A Gym Rat adds 0.9 to a growing player and 0.45 to an older one. A Coast player loses 0.8 while growing and 0.35 once past his peak. A Consummate Pro past his peak gains 0.3.
  • Morale matters a little. A young player's gain is scaled by a factor from 0.7 to 1.15 based on his morale. A miserable 21 year old who is not in the gym grows less.
  • Staff helps. A good coach adds to young players and slows the fall of older ones, but it never reverses decline. If you take the Developer skill as a GM, it adds a bonus to your young players only. AI teams do not get that bonus.

Mentors

  • A veteran in the room lifts a young player's summer. The game checks everyone on the roster and uses the best mentor. To count, the veteran must be at least 5 years older than the kid and rated 68 or higher. The kid must be below his peak age.
  • The mentor score starts from his rating and age: 0.045 per point of overall above 66, plus 0.02 per year of age above 28. A Leader adds 0.35. A Consummate Pro adds 0.40. An Alpha Dog subtracts 0.15 because he is not interested in teaching. A Cancer subtracts 0.30. A mentor at the kid's own position gets 1.35 times the score.
  • Mood scales it. The score is multiplied by the veteran's morale divided by 60, kept between 0.4 and 1.3. A sulking veteran teaches nobody. The final score is capped at 1.1.
  • The score is then multiplied by 1.5 and added to the kid's summer gain. In plain terms, a strong mentor can add about a point or more to a young player's summer.
  • Mentors also change the ceiling. If the mentor score is above 0.5 and the kid played at least 35 percent minutes share, his potential tends to rise a bit.

Pairing a mentor yourself

  • You can ask a veteran to mentor a specific kid from the player card. This only works on your own team and only for pairs the development system would read.
  • A pairing multiplies the veteran's score by 1.25 and adds 0.25. The game's own example: a 70 rated 33 year old at the kid's position scores 0.38 alone and 0.72 when paired. That is worth about half a point of overall each summer. A pairing helps a cheap 68 to 72 veteran the most. A star was already near the cap.
  • The veteran can say no. He starts at a 60 percent chance to agree. A Consummate Pro adds 35 points. A Leader adds 25. A Company Man or Grinder adds 15. An Alpha Dog subtracts 40 and a Cancer subtracts 30. A veteran on a cheap deal (under 5 percent of the cap) adds 15. His morale also shifts the odds. The final chance is kept between 5 and 98 percent.
  • Asking again does not reroll the answer. A refusal also costs him 3 morale.
  • One veteran teaches one student, and each kid has one teacher. A pairing ends when the kid is traded, released or reaches his peak age.

The pecking order and team chemistry

  • Playing kids has a cost. A veteran is 29 or older and rated 70 or higher. A kid is 24 or younger. If a kid at the same position plays at least 2 more minutes per game than the veteran, and the veteran is below his own expected minutes, the veteran is displaced. Expected minutes are 34 for an 80 or better player, 30 for 74 or better, 24 for 68 or better and 16 for everyone else. The game waits for 8 games before it reads any of this.
  • A displaced veteran sulks. His morale drifts down by about 0.35 a day, scaled by his personality. Once he falls under 40 morale the problem shows up in team chemistry: minus 3 per such veteran, minus 4.5 for a Leader or Alpha Dog, with a floor of minus 9. Your beat writer will also notice once a season.
  • There are two ways out. A Consummate Pro does not sulk. And a veteran who is mentoring the kid taking his minutes gets a small morale lift instead, so pairing a displaced veteran with his replacement is the intended fix.
  • Mentors can also help chemistry. Each content mentor (morale 50 or higher) of a kid in your rotation adds 2, up to a maximum of 4.

Summer focus

  • Summer focus lets you steer where a young player's gains go. Open the player card for one of your own players who is below his peak age and pick one of five focuses. It is free and has no pack or purchase behind it.
  • The five focuses are Shooting (midrange and three point range), Playmaking (passing, handling and feel for the game), Inside (finishing, post scoring and rebounding), Defense (perimeter and rim defense) and Body (athleticism and stamina).
  • A focus changes where he improves, not how much. The game gives a focused summer the same weighted worth as an unfocused one. Points in the focus ratings are accepted at the usual chance. Points anywhere else are accepted at only 20 percent of the usual chance.
  • Pointing a focus at ratings his position barely uses can cost a little overall. A center working on his handle needs more points to earn the same worth, up to 1.6 times the normal count, and past that the summer is worth less. So specializing can cost a bit but never adds anything.
  • A focus does nothing in a summer where he does not grow, and it never slows a decline. If every focus rating is already 99, the summer is spent the normal way.
  • A good use: pick the focus that matches what his position leans on, to push him toward a clear role. A shooting focus on a young wing builds a shooter. A playmaking focus on a guard builds a passer.

Potential is a belief

  • Potential is not fixed. Each summer the game revises a young player's ceiling, but only if he played at least 35 percent minutes share, so a real sample counts.
  • If he produced well above what his overall normally produces, by more than 3 points of overall equivalent, the ceiling tends to rise. If he produced well below, by more than 3 points, it tends to fall. A Gym Rat gets a small boost to the ceiling and a Coast player a small cut.
  • A gap that will not close is evidence the ceiling was wrong. If he is within two years of his peak age and still more than 8 points under his potential, the ceiling is cut.
  • Past his peak, the ceiling closes in on his overall. There is no upside left to sell. Potential never drops below his current overall.
  • Drafted players also carry a hidden development curve, from about minus 2 to plus 2. A positive curve is a late bloomer. He gains extra every growth summer, his ceiling climbs, and he ages later too. A negative curve is a bust. He loses ground and his ceiling falls, and he does not get a shorter prime for it. This is why a lottery pick sometimes never arrives and a second rounder sometimes becomes a star. See the draft and trade guide for scouting.

Decline, mileage and retirement

  • Mileage is a measure of career minutes on a 0 to 1 scale, where 30,000 career minutes is the top. More mileage means a shorter plateau and a steeper drop after it. If the game has no minutes on file it estimates 26 minutes per game played.
  • Good habits and staff slow decline but never reverse it. A player who barely played (under 15 percent minutes share) is fresher but also a bit rustier, which costs a small amount.
  • Nobody retires before 31. A player rated 78 or higher never retires before 34, and has only a 2 percent yearly chance from 34 to 35.
  • A veteran still worth a rotation spot, rated 68 or higher, retires later. His odds start at 9 percent per year of age past 33. A fading player starts at 11 percent per year past 31. Mileage adds up to 18 points. Players under 58 overall add 28 points more, and under 50 add another 25.
  • Other adjustments: a player rated above 74 subtracts 18 points. A Consummate Pro subtracts 6. A player 35 or older with a ring adds 10, since he has nothing left to prove. At age 40 and up it adds 55 points.
  • The league as a whole is nudged each summer so ratings do not inflate. If the league mean is above 60.5, everyone's growth is nudged down a little, and the same in reverse for a cold league. It applies to every team alike, yours included.

Loyalty is the long game

  • Morale is how a player feels this week. Loyalty is what he has decided about your team over years. It moves slowly. The bands are Lifer (82 and up), Committed (64), Professional (44), Detached (26) and Mercenary (below that).
  • It rises when a player is genuinely happy (morale 70 or higher), when you win (a win rate of 60 percent or higher), and a little each season he stays. It falls when he is miserable (morale under 35, and faster under 22), when his role is not respected, when you lose a lot (35 percent or lower), and fast during a holdout.
  • Loyalty pays off in money. It changes what a player will re-sign for, from a discount of about 19 percent at the top to a markup of 12 percent at the bottom. It also makes a player harder for other teams to pull away. Take care of a player for three years and you get something money cannot buy. See the free agency and contracts guide for how asks work.

A simple plan for a young core

  • Give your best prospects real minutes. Nothing else in the system is worth as much.
  • Keep one or two cheap veterans rated 68 or higher, at the prospect's position if you can. Ask them to mentor. A Pro or a Leader is the best choice and an Alpha Dog the worst.
  • Set a summer focus on each young player that fits his position.
  • Watch the veterans who lose minutes to kids. Pair them with their replacements before morale drops under 40.
  • Sell older stars before the cliff. Feel for the game lasts the longest, so shooters and passers hold value better than athletes.
  • If you are starting out, read the beginner's guide first.

Player development FAQ

Why is my young player not improving?

Minutes are the biggest lever. A prospect who never plays barely grows, whatever his potential says. A player who sits on the bench, has a sulking morale, or carries the Coast trait also grows less. Give him real minutes and a good mentor.

Does summer focus make a player better overall?

No. A focus changes where his gains land, not how many he gets. Most of the points go to the focus ratings and fewer go everywhere else. It only works on your own players who are still below their peak age.

How do I set up a mentor?

Open a young player's card and ask a veteran to mentor him. The veteran must be at least 5 years older, rated 68 or higher, and the kid must be below his peak age. The veteran can say no, and a refusal costs him 3 morale.

When do players start to decline and retire?

Decline begins a couple of years after a player's peak age, sooner if he has played heavy career minutes. Nobody retires before 31. A player rated 78 or higher never retires before 34. Useful veterans rated 68 or higher hang on longer than fading ones.
