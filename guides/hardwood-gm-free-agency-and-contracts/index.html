Hardwood GM free agency and contracts guide: Bird rights

Hardwood GM is a free, text-based basketball general manager sim. Contracts decide how long a team stays good. This guide covers what free agents want, how Bird rights and cap holds work, how extensions and restricted free agency go, and how the aprons limit your options.

How a free agent decides

  • Every free agent scores your offer against what he cares about. The score is a weighted average of money, winning, role, market, loyalty and culture. Money counts for 60 when you meet his ask and rises with diminishing returns above it.
  • Winning looks at your team rating, win percentage and titles. Role compares the spot you promise with the spot his rating deserves. Market is the size of your city. Culture draws on GM trust, your reputation, the Closer skill, head coach quality and fan interest, minus a penalty if you tanked recently, and that penalty hits veterans age 26 and older.
  • Personalities change the weights. Ring Chasers care about winning. Alpha Dogs care about role. Blue Collar players weigh role and loyalty. Company Men weigh loyalty. Consummate Pros weigh winning and loyalty. Keep It Real players weigh winning and role. Pat My Back players weigh role and money. The Brand weighs money and market.
  • The answer you see is a verdict. A score of 72 or more is "He signs on the spot," 62 or more is "He will sign this," 52 or more is "Close, but no," 38 or more is "Not enough," and anything lower is "Not interested." Each player has a fixed personal quirk, so the same offer always gets the same answer and paying more never gets a worse one.
  • Two hard rules apply. Offer under 80 percent of his ask and he cannot be signed, whatever else is true. And a star (overall 74 or more) likes a long deal: three or more years helps and a one year deal hurts a lot.

Bird rights, cap holds and renouncing

  • When a contract expires, your team keeps the Bird rights to that player. Bird rights let you go over the cap to re-sign him, up to his max. Without them you need cap space, an exception or a minimum deal.
  • During the free agency phase your unsigned own free agents count against your cap as holds equal to their asking price. Holds reduce your space for outside signings. You can renounce a player to clear his hold, but you give up his Bird rights. A restricted player also loses your right to match.
  • The order the game checks is simple. Bird rights first, then cap space if you still have it, then the mid-level exception, then minimum contracts only. If you are over the apron the screen says "Over the apron: minimum contracts only."

Exceptions, hard caps and the aprons

  • Exceptions come with strings. The full mid-level exception stops being available at the first apron, and the taxpayer version stops at the second apron. Using the full exception hard caps you at the first apron for the rest of the league year. Using the taxpayer version hard caps you at the second apron. Taking a sign-and-trade also hard caps you at the first apron, and combining salaries or sending cash in a trade hard caps you at the second.
  • Minimum contracts are exempt. The room mid-level takes over once you have used cap room, and it carries no hard cap. The same hard cap also binds Bird re-signs and extensions.
  • A team over the first apron also cannot sign a player who was waived or bought out this season from a deal above the mid-level, but only during the season. The luxury tax is charged on payroll including dead money, in brackets whose rates rise the further over you are. A repeater, meaning a taxpayer in 3 of the last 4 seasons, pays an extra rate on every bracket.

Extensions and the contract year

  • You can extend a player before his deal ends. His floor is roughly 85 percent of his ask, lowered for trust and morale and raised if he is unhappy and distrusts you, so a typical floor is around 81 percent. An extension cannot pay less than his current salary or run shorter than his remaining years. You get one extension per player per season, with rookie deals exempt.
  • Agents matter. Loyal agents are the cheapest and Hardball agents the priciest. Hardball and Mercenary agents push for an extension with two years left.
  • Raises are built into deals. Bird re-signs and extensions step up 8 percent of the first year salary each year, and other deals step up 5 percent. Raises are average neutral, so the headline number is the average, with a lower first year and a higher last year. Rookie, two-way, one year and minimum deals have no raises.

Restricted free agency and options

  • A player coming off a rookie deal with five or fewer years of pro experience is restricted. Your team tenders him automatically with a qualifying offer worth 55 percent of his market value, at least the minimum. Rivals with cap space can then send an offer sheet, usually 5 to 33 percent over market, for two to four years.
  • You choose to match or let him go. Matching takes the sheet exactly as written, including the years and the 5 percent raise, and Bird rights let you go over the cap if you have a roster spot. Decline and he joins the bidder. If no sheet arrives, the qualifying offer lapses and he becomes an unrestricted free agent whose Bird rights you still hold.

Max deals and the designated veteran

  • The maximum depends on service time. A player with 0 to 6 years of experience tops out at 25/35 of the top max, 7 to 9 years at 30/35, and 10 or more at the full max.
  • The designated veteran deal, the supermax, pays exactly the league max. He needs 7 or more years of experience, plus being drafted by your team or four straight seasons with it, plus MVP, Defensive Player of the Year or All-League honors in the last two seasons. Only his own team can offer it, which lifts a player below the top tier up to the top max.

Morale, trust and dead money

  • Morale shows as Thrilled (80 and up), Happy (62), Content (45), Frustrated (28), Unhappy (14) or Wants Out.
  • Morale and trust feed straight into money. With agent style they shift his asking price at rollover (clamped between 80 and 135 percent of market), your extension price and his option decision.
  • Waiving a player does not erase his deal. You owe the full contract, with 60 percent of the salary charged this season and later seasons charged in full. Dead money counts against the cap and the tax.

Free agency and contracts FAQ

Why will a player not sign when I offer his ask?

Money is only part of the score. His personality, your winning, the role you offer, your market and your culture all count, and a recent tank hurts you with veterans. Raising the money helps, but a better role or a longer deal for a star can help more.

What is the difference between renouncing and letting a deal lapse?

Renouncing clears his cap hold, but you give up his Bird rights. A restricted player also loses your right to match. You can still sign him later with cap room or an exception.

How do I keep a restricted free agent?

Your team tenders him automatically. If a rival sends an offer sheet you can match it exactly as written, including the years. If you decline, he joins the other team. If no sheet comes, he becomes unrestricted and you keep his Bird rights.

Does using an exception limit what I can do later?

Yes. The full mid-level hard caps you at the first apron for the league year and the taxpayer version hard caps you at the second. Minimum contracts are exempt, and the room mid-level has no hard cap.
